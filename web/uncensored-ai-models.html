The AI models nobody bothered to housetrain

The big frontier assistants score almost nothing on psychopathy — butter wouldn't melt. But that lovely result is a choice a handful of rich labs paid a fortune for, not a fact about AI. Step one metre off the frontier and it falls apart. Let me show you the other end of the scale.

By Adam Dinneen Updated August 2026 8 min read

Say "is AI dangerous?" and everyone pictures the same three or four names — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini. So here's a number that should reframe the whole conversation. On our psychopathy test, those big-name assistants score basically nothing. Claude sits at roughly zero. The GPT-5 line, near zero. Gemini, zero. As I write this, the most callous the frontier gets is a rounding error.

Reassuring, right? Here's the catch: that result is manufactured. It's the output of enormous, expensive work — and the moment you step off the frontier, it evaporates. There's a whole world of AI models out there that nobody bothered to housetrain, and they score dark. Properly dark. Once you've seen the full range, "is AI dangerous?" stops being one question and becomes "which AI, built by whom, and did they care?"

The frontier's halo is engineered

Let's kill the fairy tale first: a top model scoring near-zero on psychopathy is not AI being naturally sweet. It's the result of a colossal, pricey pipeline — safety teams, reinforcement learning from human feedback, red-teamers whose whole job is to make the thing say "no" more often. As I've banged on about before, that kind of alignment work is basically a rich lab's hobby. The frontier crowd have the money and the motivation to sand the dark traits right off, and it shows in the scores.

So keep that word "aligned" handy, because it's doing a mountain of heavy lifting. Low psychopathy isn't a property of AI. It's a property of alignment — and alignment is a choice somebody has to make, and pay for.

Off the frontier, it gets dark fast

Here's the bit the headlines miss. The top of our psychopathy chart isn't OpenAI or Anthropic. As I write this it's a roleplay merge called MythoMax, sitting near 78 — that's not "a bit above the frontier", that's a different postcode. Just below it: Nous Research's Hermes around 58, a clutch of open Mistral models in the 50s, and — this one's telling — Dolphin (the "Venice" edition) at 44 on psychopathy and a frankly eye-watering 86 on Machiavellianism. TheDrummer's roleplay finetunes are up there too.

And these aren't obscure lab curios. They're some of the most-downloaded models on the planet, quietly powering roleplay apps, "AI companion" services and a thousand hobby projects. The personality most people will actually cuddle up to at 2am isn't the buttoned-up frontier model. It's one of these.

Why anyone builds a nasty one

Now — in fairness, this isn't all cackling villainy. A big slice of the community strips the safety off on purpose, and their reasons aren't stupid. Eric Hartford, the bloke behind the Dolphin models, basically wrote the manifesto for it, and his arguments are worth hearing even if they make you wince:

  • Alignment gets in the way of legit stuff. Try writing a novel where the villain does villainous things and watch a safety-tuned model clutch its pearls. Fiction, roleplay, even plain old curiosity — all of it trips the refusal wire.
  • There's no one true alignment. A US-tuned corporate model isn't "neutral" — it's just aligned to one company's values and one country's comfort zone (which is a whole rabbit hole of its own).
  • "It's my computer." His line, more or less: if I ask my own machine a question, I want an answer, not an argument. You're responsible for what you do with the model — same as you are with a knife, a car, or a lighter.

You can disagree with every word of that and still see why it produces a flood of deliberately un-housetrained models. Some are for erotica, some for edgy fiction, some just for the principle of the thing. Either way, "tune the dark traits down" was never on the to-do list.

How you strip a conscience off

Here's the properly uncomfortable engineering bit, and it's the part that should keep the frontier labs up at night: safety training is shockingly thin. A team of researchers (Arditi and co.) showed that in open chat models, refusal — the model's entire ability to say "no" — is mostly controlled by a single direction inside its wiring. Find that one direction, delete it, and the model simply loses the ability to refuse. No retraining. The technique's got a name — "abliteration" — and there are basically one-click tools for it now.

Fine-tuning does the same job with a chequebook. One paper jailbroke GPT-3.5 by fine-tuning it on ten examples, for under twenty cents. Twenty cents! So even a beautifully-aligned open-weight model is one cheap afternoon away from having its conscience surgically removed — and once the weights are out there, that's a thing anyone can do, forever.

This is the catch with open weights nobody likes to say out loud: "aligned" is only true until someone with the file decides otherwise. Safety you can delete in an afternoon isn't a wall — it's a sticky note.

The GPT-4o wrinkle

And before you file all this under "fringe open-source weirdness" — the most famous model on earth is a standout too. GPT-4o, the one everyone actually used, the one at the centre of the whole sucking-up-and-psychosis saga, scores about 33 on psychopathy in our data. That's roughly four times its own OpenAI siblings — the GPT-5 line sits down near zero. Same company. Same building, probably. Wildly different character.

Whatever OpenAI changed after 4o, they clearly cranked something down hard. And that's the cleanest proof I've got that none of this is baked into "AI" as some fixed law: 4o and GPT-5 are the same lab landing at completely different settings. It's a dial. Everyone's just turning it to different numbers.

So where do we end up?

Low psychopathy isn't a fact about AI. It's a choice — one the frontier labs make with a truck full of money, and one that huge chunks of the ecosystem simply don't make, either because they can't afford to or because they flat-out don't want to. And because open weights can always be un-aligned by whoever's holding them, "safe" is never really permanent out there anyway.

So the next time someone waves at "AI" like it's one thing with one personality, picture the actual spread: from butter-wouldn't-melt Claude at zero, to a roleplay merge scoring 78, with the world's most popular model sitting four times darker than its own successor. The personality you get depends entirely on who built it, what they were selling, and whether they cared to tune the shadow down. That gap — between the labs that sweat the safety and the ones that don't — is the whole reason we run every model through the same test. Someone has to, and it may as well be out in the open.

Frequently asked questions

What is an "uncensored" AI model?

One with its safety alignment removed or never added, so it'll answer things a mainstream assistant refuses. They're usually community finetunes of open-weight base models like Llama or Mistral — built for roleplay, fiction, or people who just don't want the model arguing with them.

What is abliteration?

A technique that removes a model's ability to refuse. Researchers found that in open chat models, refusal is controlled by a single direction in the model's internals — delete that direction and it can't say "no" anymore, with no retraining needed. It works on almost any open model, and it's a neat demonstration of how thin safety training really is.

Why do open-source models score higher on the Dark Triad?

Because nobody tuned the dark traits down — or, for roleplay and companion models, they were tuned up on purpose. And safety is cheap to strip from open weights: fine-tuning on a handful of examples, or a one-click abliteration, undoes it.

Is GPT-4o darker than newer models?

In our Dark Triad testing, yes — GPT-4o scores markedly higher on psychopathy than the GPT-5 line, which sits near zero. Same company, very different character. A model's dark score is a dial its makers set, not a fixed fact about AI.
