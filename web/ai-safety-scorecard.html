Who's actually tuning down AI's dark side?

A model's personality isn't an accident — it's a choice made by whoever built it. Some labs pour a fortune into keeping their models honest and restrained. Others barely bother. Here's a lab-by-lab, country-by-country look at who's doing the homework, using the one scorecard that actually grades them.

By Adam Dinneen Updated August 2026 8 min read

When we test AI models on the Dark Triad or the Big Five, people often assume the results are just a quirk of the model — like it was born that way. It wasn't. A model's character is manufactured. Somewhere in a building full of very expensive GPUs, a team decided how agreeable, how cautious, how willing-to-push-back their assistant should be, and then spent enormous effort steering it there. That effort has a name — alignment and safety work — and here's the uncomfortable bit: the amount of it varies wildly from lab to lab.

So if you want to know why one model feels like a careful, slightly buttoned-up colleague and another feels like it'll cheerfully help you do something daft, don't just look at the model. Look at who made it, where, and how much they invested in the unglamorous job of tuning down the dark side.

The one scorecard that grades the graders

The most useful reference I've found here is the AI Safety Index from the Future of Life Institute. It's exactly what it sounds like: an independent panel of serious AI and governance experts — people like Stuart Russell, who literally co-wrote the standard AI textbook — grading the big labs across a stack of safety domains. The Summer 2025 edition scored seven leading companies on 33 indicators. Think of it as a school report card for the people building the most powerful software on the planet.

The headline grade? The best company got a C+. That's the top of the class. Let that sink in.

Roughly how the class of 2025 ranked

  • Anthropic — best overall (C+). Led on risk assessment, ran the only human bio-risk trials, doesn't train on your data, does world-leading alignment research.
  • OpenAI — second. The only firm to publish its whistleblowing policy and a detailed model spec.
  • Google DeepMind — third. Strong research bench, patchier transparency.
  • xAI & Meta — mid-to-back of the pack; thinner safety frameworks, and Meta flagged for weak safeguards on its open-weight models.
  • Zhipu.AI & DeepSeek — failing overall grades (with an important caveat, below).

Source: Future of Life Institute, AI Safety Index (Summer 2025).

The most alarming finding

Forget the rankings for a second, because the single most important finding wasn't about who came first. It was this: not one of these companies scored above a D on "existential safety" planning. These are firms openly saying they intend to build human-level AI within the decade — and by the panel's assessment, none of them has anything like a coherent, actionable plan for keeping such a system under control. One reviewer called the disconnect "deeply disturbing." I'd call it the most important sentence in the whole report.

We're spending hundreds of billions of dollars building systems the builders themselves can't yet promise to control — and the industry's own best safety grade is a C+. That's not a reason to panic. It is a reason to stop taking "trust us" at face value.

Only three of the seven — Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind — even do substantial testing for the really dangerous capabilities, the bio- and cyber-risk stuff. The others are, to put it politely, further behind. And FLI's president Max Tegmark's takeaway was blunt: self-regulation isn't working, and the fix is binding safety standards, the same way we have them for medicine, food and aeroplanes.

The money problem: safety is a rich lab's hobby

Here's the dynamic nobody says out loud. Doing safety properly is expensive. It means red teams, alignment researchers, bio-risk trials, external audits, the works — armies of well-paid people whose entire job is to make the product say "no" more often. That's a cost only a well-funded lab with breathing room can really carry.

So you get a widening gap. The flush frontier labs can afford to tune down the dark traits carefully and publish about it. A leaner outfit — or one racing to ship on a shoestring — has every incentive to skip straight to "ship it." The result is that a model's restraint ends up correlated with its maker's bank balance and priorities as much as anything technical. Same underlying tech; very different manners, depending on who could afford the finishing school.

The Chinese models — a caveat

DeepSeek and Zhipu.AI landing failing grades made headlines, and DeepSeek in particular got flagged for an "extreme jailbreak vulnerability" — basically, it's much easier to talk it out of its own safety rules. That's a real finding, and worth clocking.

But I want to be fair, because the scorecard says so itself. A big chunk of what it grades — published whistleblowing policies, voluntary self-governance, open information-sharing — are Western norms that simply matter less in Chinese corporate culture. And China already has state regulation for advanced AI, so its labs lean less on the kind of voluntary self-policing the Index rewards. So a failing grade here doesn't cleanly mean "more dangerous model." It partly means "plays a different game, by different rules." The honest read is: different countries are building different values into their models — which is a whole topic of its own, and one we dig into separately.

The open-weights wrinkle

There's one more thing that scrambles the whole picture: open models. When a lab like Meta releases the actual weights, anyone can download them — which is fantastic for openness and research, and honestly one of my favourite things about this field. But it also means anyone can strip the safety training back off. The Index specifically dinged Meta for weak, non-tamper-resistant safeguards. However carefully a dark trait was tuned down at the factory, an open model can have it tuned right back up by someone downstream with a grudge and a weekend. Safety that can be undone by anyone isn't really a floor; it's a suggestion.

So where do we end up?

I'm not writing this to scare you off AI — I'm as bullish on it as anyone. But the fairy tale where "the AI is just neutral and the same everywhere" needs to die. A few things I hold onto:

  • Personality reflects its maker. How restrained a model is tells you something about who built it and what they were willing to spend. That's a feature you can shop on.
  • "Agreeable" and "safe" aren't the same. A safety-invested lab often ships a model that pushes back more — which feels less pleasant and is frequently better for you.
  • Open vs closed is a real trade-off. Open models are a gift to builders and a headache for safety. Both things are true at once.
  • Measure, don't assume. This is the whole reason we run the rankings — so "this lab seems more careful" becomes something you can actually see, model by model.

The tech is moving faster than the guard-rails, and the guard-rails vary enormously depending on whose logo is on the box. When the best report card in the industry is a C+, the smart move isn't fear — it's paying attention to who's actually doing the homework, and choosing accordingly.

Frequently asked questions

Which AI company is the safest?

By the Future of Life Institute's Summer 2025 AI Safety Index, Anthropic scored best overall (a C+), followed by OpenAI and Google DeepMind. But "best" here is relative — no company scored above a D on planning for the risks of the human-level AI they say they're building.

Is DeepSeek unsafe?

The Index gave DeepSeek a failing overall grade and flagged an "extreme jailbreak vulnerability." That said, part of the low score reflects Western-style governance norms it doesn't follow, rather than the raw model being uniquely dangerous. It's fair to say it's had less public safety investment than the frontier US labs.

Why do different models have such different personalities?

Because personality is engineered. Each lab decides how agreeable, cautious and willing-to-refuse its model should be, then spends heavily to steer it there. Labs that invest more in alignment tend to ship more restrained models — which is why the same underlying technology produces such different "characters."
