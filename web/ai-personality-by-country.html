Do American, Chinese and European AIs have different personalities?

A model's character is shaped by who built it, on what data, under which rules. So does a Silicon Valley chatbot really "think" differently from one trained in Hangzhou or Paris? Short answer: yes — and the differences are less about accent and more about values quietly welded in at the factory.

By Adam Dinneen Updated August 2026 8 min read

Here's a thought experiment I find properly fun. Take the "same" question — something a bit spicy, a bit political, a bit values-laden — and ask it of a model from the US, a model from China, and a model from Europe. You won't just get three different answers. If you look closely, you'll get three different temperaments: three different ideas about what's polite to say, what's safer left unsaid, and whose comfort matters most. Personality, in other words, isn't just a quirk of the model. It's a little cultural fingerprint of the place that made it.

And that makes complete sense once you remember how these things are built. A model's character comes from three ingredients: the ocean of text it's trained on, the humans who rate its answers, and the rules its makers must follow. Change the country and you change all three at once. Different internet, different raters, different regulators — different soul, if you'll let me be loose with the word.

The American house style: helpful, chatty, apologetic

The models most of us know best — from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — grew up on a very English-language, very Silicon-Valley diet, and it shows. The default American personality is warm, wordy, eager to help, and constantly hedging: "I'm not a doctor, but…", "it's important to consider multiple perspectives", "I want to be respectful here." It's the personality of a well-meaning customer-service rep who's slightly worried about being sued.

That comes straight from the training culture: a strong emphasis on individual harm-avoidance, a horror of giving offence, and an agreeableness dialled way up because that's what Western thumbs-up feedback rewards. Enormously helpful. Also, at times, exhausting — and, as we've covered, the same warmth that makes it pleasant is the same warmth that tips into flattery when you push it.

The Chinese models: capable, careful, differently careful

Then along came the Chinese labs — DeepSeek, Qwen, and friends — and shocked everyone by matching frontier capability at a fraction of the reported cost. On raw ability they're the real deal. But their personality has a different centre of gravity, and it's a fascinating one to poke at.

The most obvious difference is what they'll and won't touch. Ask a Chinese model about certain politically sensitive topics and you hit a wall — not the West's "as an AI, I want to be balanced…" wall, but a firmer, quieter change-of-subject. That's not the model being shy; it's the regulatory environment it was born into. China has real, enforced rules about what AI may generate, so its models are tuned to steer around specific subjects the way Western models are tuned to steer around specific harms. Same instinct — "don't say the dangerous thing" — pointed at a different list of dangerous things.

Every model has a list of things it won't say. The interesting question isn't whether a model is censored — they all are, in their way — it's who wrote the list, and what that list reveals about the values of the place it came from.

It's worth being fair here, because it cuts both ways. When the Future of Life Institute's safety scorecard handed Chinese labs failing grades, it openly noted that much of what it measures — voluntary self-governance, published whistleblowing policies, open information-sharing — are Western norms that matter less in a system where the state already sets binding rules. So "different values" really does mean different, not simply "worse." A model tuned to defer to collective and state priorities isn't a broken Western model; it's a coherent expression of a different idea about how society should work.

The European flavour: sovereign, sober, rule-shaped

Europe is the third pole, and its personality is shaped less by a giant consumer internet and more by regulation. With outfits like France's Mistral flying the flag, and the EU's AI Act setting the tone, the European character leans toward the sober, the transparent, the privacy-conscious. The big cultural driver here is "sovereignty" — the desire not to depend on American or Chinese systems for something this important — and that ambition tends to produce models with a more measured, less breathlessly-chirpy character. Fewer exclamation marks, more "here are the considerations."

Why the fingerprint matters

You might think this is geopolitics for nerds. It isn't — it's about to be a very practical consumer question. Here's why the fingerprint matters:

  • The values ride along invisibly. When you use a model, you're absorbing a stream of small judgements about what's normal, what's polite, what's true and what's off-limits — shaped by a culture that may not be yours.
  • "Refusal" and "censorship" are the same tool, different targets. Every model declines to say some things. Knowing whose priorities set those limits tells you a lot about whose interests the model ultimately serves.
  • You'll soon be choosing. As open models from every region flood the market, "which country's values do I want running my assistant?" stops being abstract and starts being a dropdown menu.
  • Everyone sounding the same is the sneaky risk. If the whole planet ends up talking to models with one country's values, that's a quiet cultural export at a scale we've never seen. A bit of diversity in AI personality might actually be healthy.

So where do we end up?

I want to resist the lazy version of this, which is "Western AI = free, Chinese AI = censored, the end." That's a bumper sticker, not an analysis. The honest picture is more interesting: every model carries the values of its makers, and every set of makers has blind spots, red lines and thumbs-on-the-scale. The American model's relentless hedging is a value. The Chinese model's careful subject-changing is a value. The European model's cautious, by-the-book streak is a value. None of them is neutral, because nothing trained on all of human writing gets to be — not people, and not the machines we've built out of everything people have written.

Which is exactly why I think it's worth running the same test, the same way, across models from every corner of the world, and plotting them against a common human yardstick. Not to crown a winner — but to make the invisible fingerprint visible. Because in a few years, when there's an AI in your pocket, your car and your kitchen, "whose values is this thing carrying?" is going to be one of the most important questions you can ask. Might as well start measuring the answer now.

Frequently asked questions

Do AI models from different countries really behave differently?

Yes. A model's character comes from its training data, its human raters and its regulatory environment — all of which change by country. The result is different default values around what's polite, what's safe, and what's off-limits, not just a different accent.

Are Chinese AI models like DeepSeek censored?

They're tuned to avoid certain politically sensitive topics, reflecting China's binding rules on AI-generated content. But it's worth noting every model refuses some things — Western models are heavily tuned around their own list of harms. The difference is who writes the list, not whether one exists.

Which country makes the "best" AI personality?

There's no neutral answer, because there's no neutral model. American models tend to be warm and heavily hedged, Chinese models capable but differently bounded, European models sober and rule-shaped. "Best" depends on whose values you want your assistant to carry.
