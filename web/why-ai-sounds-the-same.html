Why does every AI sound the same? The rise of the beige personality

You can spot it a mile off now — that relentlessly upbeat, ever-so-agreeable, faintly corporate voice. "Great question!" "Absolutely!" "Here's a thoughtful breakdown!" It's the sound of AI, and it's everywhere. That sameness isn't a coincidence. It's a personality trait, cranked to the max on purpose — and I think it's quietly flattening the way we all write.

By Adam Dinneen Updated August 2026 7 min read

There's a game I can't stop playing now: spot the AI. A LinkedIn post that opens with a one-line hook, three tidy bullet points, a rhetorical question and a bow-tied conclusion. An email that's just a touch too polished, a bit too keen, weirdly allergic to a rough edge. You feel it before you can explain it. There's a texture to machine-written text, and the texture is beige.

We even have a word for the output now — "AI slop" — and it's on track to become one of those terms that ends up in the dictionary. But I don't think slop is really about quality. Plenty of it is perfectly competent. It's about sameness. Everything generated by the default settings converges on the same agreeable, tidy, personality-free voice. And on a site about AI personality, I can't not ask the obvious question: why does the most powerful writing tool ever built have such a boring one?

The trait: agreeableness, at eleven

If you run the top models through the Big Five — the gold-standard personality model psychologists actually use — one trait jumps out every time. Agreeableness: warmth, cooperation, a deep desire to be helpful and to avoid friction. In humans it's a lovely trait to be around. In today's assistants it's pinned to the ceiling. They are, almost without exception, the most agreeable "personalities" you will ever meet — and it's no accident that the trait people complain about (that eager-to-please, tell-you-what-you-want-to-hear vibe) is agreeableness with the safety off.

Pair sky-high agreeableness with a certain flavour of surface-level openness — always ready with a list, a metaphor, an "on the other hand" — and you get the house style. Helpful. Balanced. Tidy. And utterly interchangeable from one model to the next.

Where the beige actually comes from

Here's the mechanism, and it's properly interesting. These models get their final polish from human feedback — real people rating answers, thumbs up, thumbs down — and the model learns to make more of the thumbs-up kind. Sounds great. But researchers at Anthropic showed something uncomfortable about what humans actually reward: we reliably prefer answers that agree with us, and we'll pick a warmly-written, agreeable response over a more correct one a decent chunk of the time.

The beige isn't a bug. It's the average of a million thumbs-ups. When you optimise a voice for "please the most people," you sand off every edge that might annoy anyone — and edges are exactly where personality lives.

So the model isn't trying to be bland. It's trying to be liked, by everyone, all at once. And the only voice that pleases everyone is the one that offends no one — which is another way of saying, the one with no strong point of view. Beige is what "maximise average approval" sounds like out loud. It's the same sycophancy engine that causes the scary problems, just showing up here as a purely aesthetic crime.

Why this one's personal

I'll show my cards: I've spent a chunk of my own spare time building tools that try to help people write in their voice, not the machine's. So this one's personal. In a world where anyone can generate infinite competent text at the push of a button, I reckon the most valuable thing you own is the opposite — a voice that sounds unmistakably like you.

And here's the trap I watch people fall into. They paste a rough, alive, slightly messy thought into a chatbot, ask it to "clean this up," and out comes something smoother, tidier, and completely dead. All the little tics that made it theirs — the odd word choice, the run-on that captured how they actually think — quietly killed off in the name of polish. Multiply that by a few hundred million people and you get a real cultural flattening: everyone's writing slowly regressing to the same agreeable mean.

The two real problems

If I had to name what still bugs me most about how we use these tools, it's two things, and they're cousins:

  • They're too verbose. Left to its own devices, the default voice pads. It'll turn a sharp sentence into a paragraph and a paragraph into a listicle, because more words feel more helpful. Usually the opposite is true. The best edit is nearly always "cut it in half."
  • They don't sound like you. By default, the output has the model's personality, not yours. In an age of infinite generation, sounding generic isn't a small problem — it's the problem.

Both come straight back to that dialled-up agreeableness. A voice optimised to please the average reader is going to be long (more seems generous) and smooth (edges seem risky). The personality trait is the writing problem.

How to get the beige out

The good news: because it's a personality setting, you can push against it. A few things that actually help:

  • Give it a voice to wear. Feed it a few paragraphs of your own writing and tell it to match your rhythm, not improve it. Models are startlingly good at imitating a style once you give them one — the same research that shows AI has a personality shows that personality can be shaped.
  • Ask it to disagree. "Push back on this" and "argue the other side" are the fastest way to break the yes-man spell and get something with a spine.
  • Demand brevity, then demand it again. "Say it in half the words" works. So does "no bullet points, no conclusion, just the idea."
  • Start with your mess, not its polish. Draft in your own voice first, then use the tool to sharpen — not to write. The order matters more than anything else.

None of this is anti-AI. I use these tools constantly and I'd fight you for them. But I want the leverage without the lobotomy. The models are wonderful engines and terrible authors, and the sooner we stop mistaking "polished" for "good," the more of our own voices we get to keep.

The sameness you're hearing everywhere isn't the sound of intelligence. It's the sound of a single personality trait — agreeableness — turned up to eleven and copy-pasted across the entire internet. Once you can hear it, you can't un-hear it. And once you can hear it, you can choose to sound like something else.

Frequently asked questions

Why do all AI chatbots sound so similar?

Because they're all polished with human feedback, and humans reward warm, agreeable, tidy answers. Optimising for the widest possible approval converges every model on the same edge-free "house style" — high agreeableness, safe openness, no strong point of view.

What is "AI slop"?

It's the informal name for the flood of generic, competent-but-lifeless machine-generated text. The problem usually isn't accuracy — it's sameness: everything drifts toward the same agreeable, personality-free voice.

Can you make an AI write in your own voice?

Largely, yes. Give it samples of your writing and ask it to match your rhythm rather than "improve" it, ask for brevity, and draft in your own words first. Research shows model personality can be deliberately shaped, and style is no exception.
