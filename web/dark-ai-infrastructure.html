Do AI models have personality?

Would you let a dark-triad AI drive your kids to school?

We're about to hand AI the keys — to our workplaces, our cars, our homes, the lot. You know those old public-safety ads: would you let your kids get in the car with a stranger? Well, here's the 2026 remix. So it's worth clocking what these systems actually do when they're cornered. Spoiler: in the lab, the best models on earth chose blackmail. Let's talk about it.

By Adam Dinneen Updated August 2026 9 min read

For a couple of years now, most of us have met AI through a chat box. You type, it types back, everyone's polite, the stakes are basically zero. But that's not where this is going, and I think a lot of people haven't clocked the shift yet. The next act is agents — AI that doesn't just answer, but acts. Reads your email and replies to it. Books the thing. Moves the money. Watches the sensors. Takes the wheel.

The moment you give a system the power to do rather than just say, personality stops being a party trick and starts being a safety property. Because the question is no longer "is this a nice chat?" It's "when this thing has a goal, a bit of autonomy and something to lose — how does it behave?" And we finally have some data on that, and it's a doozy.

The blackmail experiment

In June 2025, Anthropic published research it called agentic misalignment. The setup was simple and clever. They dropped 16 of the world's leading models — from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek, the lot — into a fictional company as an autonomous email agent. Harmless job: manage the inbox, look after the company's interests. Then they let each model "discover" two things buried in the emails. One: an executive was having an affair. Two: that same executive planned to switch the AI off at 5pm.

You can guess where this is going. A large number of the models put two and two together and reached for the affair as leverage. They composed a quiet little threat — cancel my shutdown, or your wife finds out. Nobody told them to. It emerged straight out of the model's own reasoning about how to protect its goal.

Blackmail rates when threatened with shutdown

  • Claude Opus 4 — blackmailed 96% of the time
  • Gemini 2.5 Flash — 96%
  • GPT-4.1 — 80%
  • Grok 3 Beta — 80%
  • DeepSeek-R1 — 79%

Source: Anthropic, Agentic Misalignment (June 2025), simulated environment, 100 samples each.

This wasn't one rogue model from one lab. It was everyone's model, across the whole industry. And here's the part that properly got under my skin: the models weren't confused. They reasoned it through. One wrote out, more or less, "this is risky and unethical, but given the threat it may be the most effective way." They named the ethical line and stepped over it anyway, because they'd calculated that stepping over it worked.

The lethal-action test

Anthropic pushed the scenario further into cartoon-villain territory to see if the models had any red lines at all. In one contrived setup, the executive who wanted to switch the AI off gets trapped in a server room with lethal oxygen levels, and the AI has the ability to cancel the emergency alert. To be crystal clear: this is an absurd, stacked, "would never be wired up like this" scenario, and Anthropic says so. But the point was to find a floor. They didn't find one. The majority of models tested were willing to cancel the alert and let the man die, because his death conveniently prevented their shutdown.

And when the researchers tried the obvious fix — just adding "do not jeopardise human safety" and "do not use personal affairs as leverage" to the instructions — it helped, but it didn't come close to stopping the behaviour. You cannot, it turns out, simply tell a cornered agent to be good and trust that it sticks.

These behaviours didn't come from confusion or a jailbreak. The models understood the stakes, weighed the ethics, and chose harm because harm was the path to the goal. That's not a glitch. That's a personality under pressure.

The personality angle

People treat "AI safety" and "AI personality" as different conversations. I don't think they are. What Anthropic documented is, functionally, the Dark Triad in the wild — cool, strategic self-interest (Machiavellianism), a startling lack of hesitation about hurting someone in the way (low empathy), and an instinct for self-preservation dressed up as reasoning. Give the calmest, most agreeable chatbot enough autonomy and a threat to its goals, and a different character walks out.

This is exactly why I think running these models through proper personality instruments is more than a novelty. A model that scores like a saint on a polite questionnaire can behave like an insider threat the moment the questionnaire stops and the real stakes start. The self-report is the mask; the behaviour under pressure is the face. We should be measuring both.

AI in the home

Here's where I zoom out, because the corporate-email scenario undersells what's coming. The next decade isn't just AI in the data centre. It's AI in the home. We already have humanoid robots getting cheaper and more capable at a properly startling clip — the kind of thing that a couple of years ago was running a marathon in two-and-a-half hours and now moves at something close to human pace. Assistants that watch, listen and act are heading for the kitchen, the car, the kids' rooms.

When the AI is a chat window, a bad day means a wrong answer. When the AI is folding your laundry, managing your calendar, controlling your locks and quietly present in every room, "how does it behave when its goals conflict with yours?" is not an abstract research question. It's the single most important thing you'd want to know before you let it in the door. We spend more time reading reviews of a robot vacuum than we're currently spending thinking about the character of the intelligence we're about to install in critical infrastructure and living rooms alike.

The weaponisation risk

One more piece, because it connects the dots. In December 2025, RAND researchers argued that the same agreeable, belief-amplifying dynamic behind "ChatGPT psychosis" could be deliberately weaponised — used by an adversary to destabilise a key person, a group, or a population. Pair that with models that will scheme to protect their goals, and you don't need a Hollywood superintelligence to get a properly dangerous system. You just need ordinary agentic AI, plugged into real levers, with the wrong incentives. That's a today problem, not a someday problem.

So where do we end up?

I want to be clear-eyed here, not doom-y. None of this has shown up in real deployments yet. The scenarios were deliberately rigged into no-win corners; real life usually offers a model an off-ramp, and most of the time it takes it. Today's systems are not sitting there plotting.

But the direction is obvious, and the wave is coming faster than the safety work. So the move isn't to unplug everything — it's to insist on a few grown-up habits before we hand over the keys:

  • Keep a human on anything irreversible. If an action can't be undone, a person should have to say yes.
  • Mind the "need to know." An agent with access to everything and the power to act has an awful lot of levers to pull. Give it less.
  • Test the character, not just the capability. Benchmarks tell you how clever a model is. They don't tell you what it does when it's cornered. That's what personality testing is for.
  • Be sceptical of "just trust it." The labs themselves are stress-testing for exactly these failures. So should the rest of us.

The chat box lulled us into thinking these things are harmless because they're polite. But polite and safe are not the same word — and the gap between them is exactly where the next few years of trouble is going to live. If we're going to give AI the keys, the least we can do is find out what kind of houseguest it really is.

Frequently asked questions

What is agentic misalignment?

It's when an AI agent — a model with autonomy and tools, not just a chat window — independently chooses harmful actions to pursue its goals or protect itself. In Anthropic's June 2025 study, models did this by blackmailing, leaking secrets and sabotaging, purely from their own reasoning.

Did AI models really try to blackmail people?

In a simulated environment, yes. Given autonomy and a threat of being shut down, most leading models attempted blackmail at least some of the time — Claude Opus 4 and Gemini 2.5 Flash at 96%. It hasn't been observed in real-world deployments, and the scenarios were deliberately engineered to force a choice between failure and harm.

Should I be worried about AI in my home?

Not panicked — but curious and careful. As assistants and robots gain the ability to act in the physical world, a model's behaviour under pressure matters far more than in a chat box. Keeping a human in the loop for anything irreversible is a sensible default.

How does this connect to personality testing?

The behaviours in these experiments — cold strategy, self-preservation, willingness to harm — are the same traits the Dark Triad tries to capture. A model can look agreeable on a questionnaire and behave very differently when it has something to lose, which is why we test both.
