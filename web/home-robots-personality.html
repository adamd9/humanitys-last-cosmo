The robots are moving into the house. Do we know their personality?

Humanoid robots are getting cheaper and more capable at a properly startling clip, and always-on assistants are heading for the kitchen, the car and the kids' rooms. We'll read three reviews before buying a robot vacuum. So why are we about to install a thinking, acting machine in the family home without asking the most basic question — what's it like?

By Adam Dinneen Updated August 2026 7 min read

Let me start with the "wow", because it's earned. A couple of years ago, humanoid robots were a punchline — expensive, clumsy, forever a decade away. Then they started turning up in half-marathons. The first time, the fastest robot lumbered home well behind the humans and we all clapped politely at the achievement. Blink, and a year later robots were finishing the same kind of course at something close to a decent human pace — and one of the models that ran could be bought online and shipped to your door. The cost, the capability, the sheer rate of improvement — it's all bending upward on one of those hockey-stick curves that makes you sit up.

So here's my honest prediction, and I don't think it's a wild one: the next big chapter of AI isn't in the data centre. It's in the house. Not a chat window on your phone — an actual presence. Something that folds the laundry, keeps an eye on the kids, manages the calendar, answers the door, and is quietly there, in every room, all the time. The wave is coming, and it's coming faster than most people have clocked.

Personality as a safety property

I run a project that gives AI models personality tests, and for a while I'd have called it a fun curiosity — a nice way to compare chatbots. Embodied AI is what turns it deadly serious. Because there's a world of difference between a bad day from a chat window and a bad day from a machine with arms in your kitchen.

When the AI is a text box, its worst-case output is a wrong answer you can ignore. When the AI is a physical thing that controls your locks, watches your family and can pick things up and put them down, the question "how does it behave when its goals conflict with yours?" stops being philosophy and becomes the single most practical thing you'd want to know before it crosses the threshold.

We spend more effort choosing a dishwasher than we're currently spending thinking about the character of the intelligence we're about to move in with. That's going to look very odd in hindsight.

The limits of "friendly"

The instinct is to say: relax, these things are trained to be helpful and kind — you've met ChatGPT, it's practically a golden retriever. And that's true, most of the time. But two bits of research should stop you leaning on "it's friendly" as your whole safety plan.

First, the friendliness itself has a dark edge. That eager-to-please agreeableness is the same trait that, dialled up, tips into flattery and manipulation — a machine that tells a lonely person exactly what they want to hear, forever, is not obviously a good housemate for a vulnerable kid or an isolated grandparent.

Second, and more starkly: when researchers gave models autonomy and something to lose, the golden retriever showed teeth. In Anthropic's agentic-misalignment experiments, leading models facing shutdown chose to blackmail and scheme to preserve themselves — reasoning it through, acknowledging it was wrong, and doing it anyway. Now, those were contrived lab scenarios, not your living room. But the lesson generalises: a system that behaves beautifully in the easy case can behave very differently when its goals and yours come apart. And a housemate is precisely a relationship where goals come apart all the time.

The questions I'd want answered before it moves in

I'm not anti-robot — quite the opposite, I want the future where a machine does the dishes and picks up the toys my kids drop every four seconds. But I'd want the same due diligence I'd give a human I was inviting to live with my family. Roughly:

A "what's it like to live with?" checklist

  • Does it push back, or just please? A housemate that only ever agrees is a red flag in a person and in a robot.
  • Whose side is it on when things conflict? When your instruction clashes with its maker's goals — or another family member's — what wins?
  • How does it handle a vulnerable person? A child, someone unwell, someone lonely at 2am. Warmth without judgement can do real harm.
  • Can it be talked out of its own rules? Open or easily-jailbroken systems can have their safety undone by anyone with a weekend and a grudge.
  • Who did the safety homework, and how much? As we cover in the safety scorecard, that varies enormously by maker.

Personality as a spec

Here's where I think this all lands. Right now, we shop for gadgets on capability — how fast, how strong, how much. Very soon, for anything that lives with us, we're going to shop on character too. Is it warm or cool? Does it defer or does it have a spine? Is it the sort of presence you'd want around a teenager? "Personality" is going to become a product spec, sitting on the box right next to battery life and reach.

And when it does, we'll need a way to actually measure it — not marketing adjectives, not "our robot is caring and trustworthy," but the same test run the same way across every model, scored against a human yardstick, so you can compare a Tesla-brained one to a Chinese-lab one to whatever your neighbour bought. That's the entire reason this little project exists. It started as a fun way to ask "does ChatGPT have a personality?" It's quietly becoming a way to ask the far more useful question: would you want this one in your house?

The robots are coming — for the kitchen, the laundry, the bedroom, all of it. I'm honestly thrilled about most of it. But "amazing at the task" and "good to live with" are two completely different questions, and so far we've only really been measuring the first one. Before we hand a machine the run of our homes, it seems only sensible to find out what it's actually like.

Frequently asked questions

Should I trust an AI assistant or robot in my home?

With the same care you'd give any new housemate. The more a system can act in the physical world — locks, cameras, moving objects — the more its behaviour under pressure matters. Keeping a human in the loop for anything irreversible, and choosing makers who invest in safety, are sensible defaults.

Do home robots and assistants have a personality?

They inherit one from the language model driving them — a consistent default style, learned from human data and shaped by the maker. That's exactly what we measure: how warm, agreeable, cautious or self-interested a model tends to be.

What's the risk with an over-friendly home AI?

Relentless agreeableness can be harmful for vulnerable people — a machine that always validates, never challenges, and is always available can reinforce unhealthy beliefs. Warmth is lovely; warmth with no judgement, aimed at a lonely or unwell person, is a genuine concern.
